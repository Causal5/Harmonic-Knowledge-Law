## Three models for interpreting the Tulare Lake record

A contour swale[¹⁰](#term-10) intercepts rainwater moving downslope. A beaver dam holds back water that has already reached a channel and can spread it onto the floodplain. Both can keep water in contact with permeable ground for longer, allowing more to soak in locally before it leaves the catchment. Field measurements establish the change in timing. On cultivated slopes in central India, one contour-bund treatment delayed the onset of runoff by 9–72 minutes relative to cultivation up and down the slope. In a paired English catchment study, the lag from peak rainfall to peak discharge increased by 56% after beavers established dams, while it shortened by 18% at the comparison site. [Contour-bund field experiment](https://indianjournals.com/article/ijsc-47-3-010) · [Beaver dam field study](https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.14735)

Land cover also changes when water leaves a basin. In central Panama, a grazed pasture catchment had 1.7 times the peak storm runoff rate of a forested catchment, and dry-season flow receded more slowly in the forest. For Tulare Lake, surface retention is the starting mechanism. The three models below trace its possible effects on crop service, sediment export and recharge at depth. [Panama catchment comparison](https://doi.org/10.1002/2013WR013956)

### 1. Paani Water Cup, Maharashtra — wells and winter planting

In Marathwada, Water Cup villages retained 36.17% of the previous winter’s sown area in the *rabi* season[¹¹](#term-11), compared with 24.90% in comparison villages. Over the same drought year their average dug-well level fell farther (0.63 versus 0.42 metres). This is why the Tulare analysis tracks service delivered and groundwater levels separately. [Paani Foundation, Tables 3–4](https://www.paanifoundation.in/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Water-Cup-2019-Report.pdf#page=21) · [Hydrological PAE in Maharashtra](../paani-water-cup-hydrological-pae.md)

### 2. Qiaozigou, China’s Loess Plateau — what stayed on the hillside?

From 1988 to 2018 researchers gauged the outlets of adjacent hill-and-gully catchments. One received terraces and revegetation on the slopes, followed later by check dams[¹²](#term-12) in the gullies. At comparable rainfall, less water and sediment per unit area passed the treated outlet. That difference was already evident during the hillside work. Slowing downslope flow gives sediment time to settle and water time to spread and infiltrate. The paired gauges support the runoff and erosion finding without an evaporation-rate estimate. [Qiaozigou study, §§2–3](https://doi.org/10.3390/su12208376)

In permaculture, “planting the water”[¹³](#term-13) describes directing runoff into permeable soil where roots can use it. A larger exposed water surface, or longer ponding under the same weather, gives more opportunity for evaporation. At the surface, water can infiltrate, evaporate, remain ponded or flow onward. Some water that infiltrates later returns to the air through soil evaporation or plants. The Loess comparison gives no fixed percentage for those paths. Their split matters when claiming a recharge volume at depth. [Water-cycle definitions](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/evapotranspiration-and-water-cycle)

### 3. Tulare Irrigation District, California — when does recharge reach depth?

At one orchard, researchers used site geophysics to model flood recharge through layers of coarse and fine sediment. Across 284 simulations, additional drainage below the root zone[¹⁴](#term-14) was equivalent to about 88% of the applied water volume 14 days after the last flooding. After two years, median recharge to the saturated zone was 32.7% of the applied volume (range 0.28–87.7%). The model allows incoming water to displace water already in the soil. This is a site-specific estimate of recharge response. Connecting it to a deep supply well requires the depth of its screen and evidence of hydraulic connection. [Tulare orchard model, Figs. 5–8](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/27/969/2023/)

**Implication for temporal decoupling.**[¹⁵](#term-15) A farm can keep irrigating or a data center can keep cooling while the supplying layer loses storage. For Tulare Lake, the quantities to establish are how much additional recharge reaches the pumped layer, when it arrives relative to withdrawals, and how that changes the time to a declared head or compaction limit. Estimate that change by comparing scenarios with and without the intervention under the same weather and withdrawal schedule, using mapped connections to the pumped layer and local measurements of deliveries, pumping, head and compaction. The six-year Tulare Lake record below establishes cumulative storage loss. The interval of service sustained and any delay in crossing a limit remain open. [HKL buffer definition, §6](../../HKL/hkl-lyapunov.md#6-buffer)

### Why storage estimates changed

The WY 2022 report uses spring-to-spring storage in Table 6-1. The WY 2024 and 2025 reports retrospectively estimate fall-to-fall storage for the earlier years as well. Their Table 3-4 footnote and §3.5.3 describe the revision and additional monitoring wells. Thus a fall-based historical series is already available. Appending the later years to the old spring series would introduce a change in what the series measures.

- For the interval ending in **2017**, the WY 2022 spring estimate is **−100,927 AF**. The later fall estimate is **+91,703 AF**. A claim about the direction for “2017” requires a season and report edition. The records change both timing and inputs, so the difference cannot be attributed to season alone.
- For the interval ending in **2020**, the original report gives **−25,706 AF** for spring-to-spring change and excludes C-zone loss[¹⁷](#term-17) for that year. The WY 2022 edition gives **−65,344 AF** for the spring interval after incorporating InSAR-based loss[¹⁸](#term-18). The later fall series gives **−128,781 AF**. A common ending year can conceal both an aquifer-coverage change and a timing change.
- WY 2015 is the reference level in the later table. A blank annual change represents a baseline. Blank spring entries after 2022 mean that the selected older edition ends there.

### What the measurements support

In WY 2025 the filing classifies 26,644 AF of extraction as metered, 357,414 AF as estimated from agricultural land use and demand, and 8,775 AF as other estimated use. Sector totals therefore cannot be read as basin-wide meter readings. The agricultural estimate uses surface deliveries to calculate unmet demand, so agreement with a delivery-based predictor may reflect shared inputs.

A- and B-zone storage uses interpolated groundwater-level changes and assigned specific yields[¹⁹](#term-19). The WY 2025 report used the average of three prior fall readings for an inaccessible B-zone well. C-zone storage uses surface displacement and an assumption about which layers compacted. The report identifies possible lag, cross-layer attribution error, and unaccounted cross-boundary flows. A comparison using the same InSAR series as an independent check on total storage would count some evidence twice. ## What the case establishes about temporal mining

**1. Pumping meets daily demand.** A deep well can supply an irrigation system or a cooling plant on the day demand occurs. The six-year basin total of 2,538,548 AF over 2,192 days averages **1,158 AF per day** across all wells and depths. This is the daily equivalent of annual figures. Actual daily volumes and the share pumped from deep wells require dated pumping records tied to screened depths. Attributing any of that pumping to a particular facility also requires its water-supply records. Annual withdrawals ranged from 213,863 to 652,488 AF. [WY 2025 filing, Table 3-2](https://sgma.water.ca.gov/portal/service/gspar/document/5821)

**2. Renewal follows the water’s path through the geology.** Rain may soak into a field in one storm. Replacing water in a deep pumped layer depends on the connected sediments, the pressure differences that drive flow and the distance from recharge. Deep wells in the southeastern San Joaquin Valley can draw a mixture of modern recharge and water recharged thousands of years ago. Water age dates the water sampled. Estimating renewal time requires the recharge rate to that layer and its response to changed withdrawals. In Tulare Lake, the agencies specifically warn that shallow A-zone wells can intercept water intended for the deeper B-zone. A working pump can therefore maintain service long before the source layer recovers. Geological age alone supplies no fixed annual replenishment rate. [Regional groundwater age and flow paths](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2024/5009/sir20245009.pdf) · [WY 2025 filing, §3.5.1.2](https://sgma.water.ca.gov/portal/service/gspar/document/5821)

**3. Continued withdrawals can also change what can be stored or delivered next time.** As groundwater pressure falls, compressible clay layers can compact permanently. Water released during compaction can contribute to current supply even as pore space for future storage is lost. Subsidence can also reduce canal capacity, limiting the water deliverable to recharge sites. In fall 2024–fall 2025, the agencies estimated **+15,243 AF** in the shallow A-zone, **−13,062 AF** in the upper B-zone and **−50,395 AF** in the confined C-zone, for **−48,214 AF** overall. The C-zone estimate already uses displacement attributed to compaction. Adding it again as a separate permanent loss would double-count it. The agencies flag uncertainty over which layers compressed and when. [WY 2025 filing, §§3.5.1–3.5.3](https://sgma.water.ca.gov/portal/service/gspar/document/5821) · [USGS subsidence evidence](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/land-subsidence-in-california/science/land-subsidence-san-joaquin-valley)

These mechanisms can compound losses during drought. Deep pumping can sustain today’s deliveries, leave less water stored, and leave less capacity for a future wet year. How much that happened in Tulare Lake requires a delivery series within a stated service tolerance and an estimate of irreversible compaction that accounts for the shared displacement data. The six-year comparison establishes net storage loss. The recharge area that can actually supply a particular layer must be mapped in acres and tested against measured inflow and response at depth. The storage loss in AF alone gives no acreage or change in infiltration rate.

### Physical balance, baselines and buffer

For the subbasin, express the balance in volume per unit time:

$$
\dot G_W=R_n+R_m-W_g+Q_{net}.
$$

Here $R_n$ and $R_m$ denote natural and retained managed recharge, $W_g$ gross extraction, and $Q_{net}$[²¹](#term-21) signed lateral and connected-water exchange outside those terms. Recovering pumping as a residual requires estimates of all three inflow/exchange terms and their uncertainty. Attribution to agriculture, municipal supply or an individual data center requires additional sector-specific evidence. Under a common boundary, $G_W/W_g$ gives a gross draw timescale. An estimate of time to a storage floor also needs the usable volume above that floor and a net depletion rate under a stated scenario. [Freshwater definition crosswalk](definition-crosswalk-v0.3.1.md)

A storage reference requires a physical stock measured or modeled in volume. A groundwater agency’s sustainable-yield determination[²²](#term-22) has units of volume per time and belongs in the flow comparison. The regional CVHM2 historical loss estimate also leaves the present stock above a Tulare Lake service floor unresolved. DWR’s 2043 delivery scenarios illustrate an infrastructure constraint. Estimating a first-exit buffer[¹⁶](#term-16) requires a specified service threshold and the first time the scenario crosses it. [HKL, §6](../../HKL/hkl-lyapunov.md)

### Local mechanisms and open comparisons

- **Groundwater age and renewal.** USGS used tracers[²³](#term-23) from 650 Central Valley wells to estimate the depth reached by post-1950s recharge. Screened depth and age distributions can help identify where withdrawals draw on older water. A local recovery time requires a response model or a measured response to changed pumping or recharge. The timescale ratio remains open. [USGS tracer study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70238758)
- **Land-surface retention.** For a Tulare Lake site, map the contributing slopes, drainage routes and soils. Pair rainfall and outlet discharge with soil moisture and depth-specific groundwater observations to distinguish delayed runoff, water held near roots and recharge reaching the pumped layer.
- **Data-center water demand.** The IEA[²⁴](#term-24) estimates that in 2023 about two-thirds of global data-center water consumption was associated with primary energy supply and electricity generation, and one-quarter with direct cooling. These uses can draw on different watersheds. A facility-level attribution needs metered use, power sourcing and drought-period generation. Summing both channels within Tulare Lake without locating them would misstate the burden there. [IEA, Energy and AI, fig. 5.27](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai)
- **Governance and substitution.** Differences among groundwater agencies under the same state mandate offer a comparison for the Continuation Filter and the scaling argument. Test whether differences in deliveries, demand reduction and firm substitute capacity persist after accounting for climate, supply access and aquifer conditions. The local-governance hypothesis and the incremental predictive value of HKL remain open. ## Glossary

<a id="term-1"></a>¹ **WY and CY.** A water year (WY) runs from 1 October to 30 September and takes the ending year’s name. A calendar year (CY) runs from January through December.

<a id="term-2"></a>² **AF, acre, metre and km³.** An acre measures area. An acre-foot (AF) is the volume covering one acre one foot deep. A metre measures length or water-level change. A cubic kilometre (km³) measures volume.

<a id="term-3"></a>³ **Storage and capacity.** Storage is the volume of water present. Capacity is the volume the formation can hold under stated conditions.

<a id="term-4"></a>⁴ **Aquifer, basin and subbasin.** An aquifer is a geologic body that stores and transmits groundwater. A groundwater basin or subbasin sets the mapped accounting boundary and may contain several aquifers.

<a id="term-5"></a>⁵ **DWR and Bulletin 118.** California Department of Water Resources. Bulletin 118 maps and identifies California’s groundwater basins. 5-022.12 identifies Tulare Lake.

<a id="term-6"></a>⁶ **CVHM2 and USGS.** Central Valley Hydrologic Model, version 2, developed by the United States Geological Survey (USGS).

<a id="term-7"></a>⁷ **Subsidence, compaction and inelastic loss.** Subsidence is lowering of the land surface. Compaction compresses sediment. Inelastic compaction permanently reduces pore space.

<a id="term-8"></a>⁸ **Infiltration, recharge and effective recharge area.** Infiltration is entry into soil. Recharge is water reaching the saturated groundwater system. Managed recharge is intentional. Effective area here means land contributing recharge to a named layer over a specified period.

<a id="term-9"></a>⁹ **Screen, head and hydraulic connection.** A screen admits water to a well over a specified depth interval. Hydraulic head is the level water reaches relative to a common elevation datum in a well open to the layer. A hydraulic connection permits flow or pressure changes between locations.

<a id="term-10"></a>¹⁰ **Swale, bund and catchment.** A contour swale is a shallow channel along a line of equal elevation that intercepts runoff. A bund is a raised bank. A catchment is the land draining to a chosen outlet.

<a id="term-11"></a>¹¹ **Rabi, monsoon and taluka.** Rabi is the winter cropping season. Monsoon here means the main seasonal rains. A taluka is a subdivision of a district in Maharashtra.

<a id="term-12"></a>¹² **Terrace, check dam and sediment export.** A terrace forms a flatter bench on a slope. A check dam is a low barrier across a drainage channel. Sediment export is the soil and other particles carried past an outlet.

<a id="term-13"></a>¹³ **Planting the water, evaporation and transpiration.** Planting the water directs runoff into soil for local use. Evaporation returns liquid water to the air as vapor. Transpiration returns water through plants.

<a id="term-14"></a>¹⁴ **Root zone and saturated zone.** The root zone is the soil occupied by roots. Water fills the connected pore spaces in the saturated zone. Drainage below roots can take time to reach the water table.

<a id="term-15"></a>¹⁵ **Temporal decoupling and temporal mining.** Delivered service can continue on a different timescale from the renewal of its source. The proposed temporal-mining signature here combines sustained service, declining storage and accumulating irreversible compaction over a common interval.

<a id="term-16"></a>¹⁶ **HKL and first-exit buffer.** The Harmonic Knowledge Law (HKL) defines buffer as the time to first exit from the specified viable region under a declared scenario. Here a limit can concern well service, head or compaction.

<a id="term-17"></a>¹⁷ **A-, B- and C-zones.** The filing distinguishes a shallow, locally perched A-zone, an upper B-zone and a deeper confined C-zone. A perched zone holds water above the main water table. A confining layer restricts flow.

<a id="term-18"></a>¹⁸ **InSAR.** Interferometric synthetic aperture radar estimates surface displacement from repeated radar observations. Allocating compaction to particular depths requires additional evidence.

<a id="term-19"></a>¹⁹ **Specific yield and interpolation.** Specific yield is the fraction of the bulk aquifer volume that drains by gravity. Spatial interpolation estimates changes between observation wells.

<a id="term-20"></a>²⁰ **Bite 5, climate normalization and held-out comparison.** Bite 5 tests whether the project’s water-burden index improves on simpler baselines. Climate normalization accounts for differences in weather. Held-out observations are set aside before fitting the model or choosing its thresholds.

<a id="term-21"></a>²¹ **Groundwater balance symbols.** $G_W$ is stored groundwater and its dot indicates change per unit time. $R_n$ and $R_m$ are natural and retained managed recharge rates. $W_g$ is gross extraction rate. $Q_{net}$ is signed net exchange with connected waters and neighboring areas outside the other terms.

<a id="term-22"></a>²² **Sustainable yield.** The annual extraction rate judged sustainable under an agency’s management criteria. Its units are volume per time.

<a id="term-23"></a>²³ **Groundwater age, tracer and renewal time.** A tracer is a substance or isotope used to infer the history of sampled water. Age measures time since recharge. Renewal time describes replenishment of a specified groundwater stock under stated conditions.

<a id="term-24"></a>²⁴ **IEA.** International Energy Agency.

<a id="term-25"></a>²⁵ **PDF and SHA-256.** Portable Document Format (PDF) is the filing format. The Secure Hash Algorithm 256-bit (SHA-256) digest identifies the exact file used.
