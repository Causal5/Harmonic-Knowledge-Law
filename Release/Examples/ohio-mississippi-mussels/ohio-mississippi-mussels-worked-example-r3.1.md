## Executive proposition

The Ohio–Mississippi freshwater-mussel history illustrates a defensible structural claim: **agentic activity can remove a slowly replaced biological stock, interrupt its regenerative dependencies, and alter the ecological functions and response options available to connected systems. Subsequent corrective work may then be needed to accomplish parts of what the original assemblage reproduced or sustained without deliberate human intervention.** The claim concerns direction and mechanism; it does **not** require an invented regional percentage loss, a single collapse curve, or a complete reconstruction of every ecological interaction. [1–4, 10–12]

The biological evidence supports changes in particular *native* functions and reproduction pathways, not a theorem that all aquatic functions diminished. Invasive bivalves, for example, can continue filtering water while reorganizing the ecology of the receiving system. Thus replacement of native mussels is **not equivalent** to recovery of the native assemblage. [11, 13]

## 1. Boundary and what the case establishes

**Geography and interval.** The historical case centers on the Upper Mississippi, Ohio, Illinois, Wabash, and Tennessee river systems, with the Great Lakes included when discussing zebra-mussel arrival and regional invasion. Observations come from different rivers and eras. They are presented as **linked mechanisms**, not one controlled basin-wide experiment. [1, 2]

**Before.** Native unionid beds consisted of long-lived mussels with fish-dependent larval stages. They provided water-column filtration, moved particulate and dissolved nutrients through benthic food webs, and contributed physical habitat through burrowing and shell structure. Coexisting species were not functionally identical; temperature-dependent functional guilds have been demonstrated experimentally. [3, 11–13]

**Intervention.** Commercial shell harvesting rapidly removed reproductive adults in heavily exploited beds. River engineering changed channel habitat and obstructed particular host-fish migrations. Later human-mediated introductions added zebra-mussel biofouling and, in portions of the Mississippi basin, black-carp predation. These processes differ in causal origin, location, and timing, and must not be treated as interchangeable. [1, 2, 4, 7–10]

**After.** At documented sites, populations declined, juvenile recruitment was disrupted, or native community composition changed. In some restoration programs, people now collect host fish, infect them with glochidia under managed conditions, rear juveniles, protect them in culture systems, and stock or translocate animals. Those actions demonstrate *attempted corrective effort* and its complexity, but not automatically that a particular restoration program succeeded or that it completely replaced an original ecological process. [4–6, 14]

## 2. The minimum physical argument: depletion and reproductive debt

A bounded stock relationship is sufficient for the initial point:

> **Change in native stock = successful native recruitment − harvest − other losses**, with immigration and emigration added when relevant to the chosen river reach.

When losses persistently exceed successful replacement, stock falls. Historical commercial depletion and relocation into other harvesting areas are reported in the accompanying historical synthesis and documented in the literature on mussel exploitation. No precise universal "10–50 times recruitment" multiplier is required or adopted. [1, 2]

A second, more revealing failure can occur *before* adult animals disappear. The life cycle of many native species requires a compatible fish host for larval development and dispersal. If that link is severed, long-lived adults can remain visible for decades while new recruitment is greatly reduced. This is **demographic or reproductive debt**: retained adult stock temporarily conceals diminished future replacement capacity. [3, 4]

**The skipjack case.** The National Park Service explains that the 1913 Keokuk dam obstructed upstream migration of skipjack herring in the Upper Mississippi. Skipjack herring are suitable hosts for ebonyshell and elephant-ear mussels; controlled experiments confirmed those host relationships. The case shows a concrete pathway from engineered river connectivity to a constrained reproductive process. It should not be recast as proof that every dam has the same effect or that skipjack is the only conceivable host under every experimental condition. [4, 5]

## 3. Two distinct invasive pressures and their interaction

### 3.1 Zebra mussels: biofouling and ecological substitution

Zebra and quagga mussels can attach to native mussel shells, interfering with feeding and movement. Their effects are spatially heterogeneous: research in the Great Lakes documented pronounced biofouling in some settings and low-biofouling refugia in others. **Filtration by an invader does not demonstrate restoration of the original native reproductive or functional network.** [8, 13]

### 3.2 Black carp: additional predation on native mollusks

Black carp (*Mylopharyngodon piceus*) first entered the United States in the early 1970s as a contaminant in imported grass carp stocks sent to an Arkansas fish farm; a second, deliberate importation in the early 1980s supplied them as a food fish and as biological control for snail-borne trematode parasites in cultured catfish. The first recorded escape was in 1994, when floodwater overtopped aquaculture ponds near the Osage River, Missouri. [31] USGS research confirms established, naturally reproducing populations in parts of the Mississippi River basin; a 2026 USGS-associated study further characterizes reproduction in the growing invasive population. Adult black carp are bottom-feeding molluscivores with pharyngeal teeth adapted for crushing shells and a mouth much larger than that of most native mollusk-eating fishes; controlled feeding studies also show juvenile carp consuming small native mussels, including *Lampsilis* species. [7, 9, 10, 32]

This is **direct evidence of the predation pathway**, not a measured basin-wide black-carp-attributable collapse. The appropriate inference is that a human-introduced predator poses an additional threat to native mussel populations—both recruits and, as the carp grow, larger animals—in locations where black carp and susceptible native populations overlap. It must be distinguished from zebra-mussel attachment and food-web restructuring. [7, 9]

A particularly relevant causal distinction is that black carp were introduced to solve an aquaculture-management problem, not to restore native unionid communities. Their escape and establishment exemplify how an action directed toward one local objective can create burdens outside that local objective's boundary. That is an **HKL interpretation of the documented facts**, not a quantified attribution of all downstream losses. [7, 15]

### 3.3 Asymmetric predation on an already decoupled native assemblage

Black carp are an additional, self-propagating molluscivorous pressure introduced into a system already affected by commercial depletion, river engineering, habitat degradation, and invasive bivalves. Their predation is not restricted to native mussels; zebra mussels and other nonnative mollusks are also susceptible. But comparable predation pressure need not produce comparable population consequences. **Comparable additional mortality can impose more persistent demographic consequences on a population whose successful recruitment is already constrained by host availability, delayed maturation, or habitat impairment. The size of that asymmetry depends on realized recruitment and mortality, not on fecundity alone.** This subsection states the mechanism, the evidence for each link, and what remains unmeasured.

**Where the renewal asymmetry actually lies.** It is not in egg or larval output. Annual fecundity of North American unionids spans nearly four orders of magnitude, from under 2,000 to about 10 million per female, so many natives match or exceed the roughly one million eggs per year a mature zebra mussel can produce. [20, 29] The difference lies in what happens after release:

- *Host dependence.* With few exceptions, unionid larvae must encyst on a compatible host fish to transform; zebra-mussel veligers drift freely and settle on any hard surface without a host. [3, 20] For ebonyshell and elephant-ear above Keokuk, the host pathway itself is obstructed. [4, 5]
- *Generation time.* Zebra mussels may mature in their first year and usually do by their second. [20] Unionid age at maturity ranges from about one to two years in some species to three to nine years in others within one assemblage, and four to six years in four New River species. [21, 22]
- *Impaired conditions.* Habitat alteration and water-quality degradation are commonly invoked stressors on juvenile survival and growth, but their role is site-specific. One review found many commonly invoked factors poorly supported as explanations for enigmatic declines, and an in-river study found invasive *Corbicula* abundance, not pollution, best explained differences in native juvenile growth. [19, 26] These conditions belong in a site's burden accounting where they are measured, not as a general assumption.

**Which size classes are exposed.** Juvenile black carp take a wide range of small mollusks; vulnerability is governed by shell strength, shape, and size relative to the predator. Age-1 carp readily consumed small *Lampsilis*, and the only tested prey they struggled with was the thick-shelled invasive *Corbicula*. [9] In a size-specific study, 95% of unattached zebra mussels were crushed or consumed, while byssally attached zebra mussels survived at a 46% higher rate. [23] Adult black carp are larger, primarily molluscivorous, and have a much larger gape than most native mollusk-eating fishes, so the size refuge available to larger natives shrinks as the carp population grows and ages. [32] Field data are consistent with this: carp longer than 791 mm showed higher occurrence of a mollusk-derived parasite than smaller fish. [30] Predation can therefore reach **both recruits and the standing adult stock**.

**Field evidence so far.** The first U.S. examination of wild black carp digestive tracts (109 fish over eight years) found bivalves in 22.8% and snails in 16.5% of non-empty stomachs, identified seven unionid taxa from shell fragments—all from soft-mud or sand-silt habitats—and found that individual fish often contained large numbers of one or two locally abundant items. [24] A follow-up study treated a fluke that lives in mollusks and persists in carp intestines as an indicator of earlier mollusk meals; on that basis, mollusk occurrence rose from 26.6% to 54.1%, so the earlier figures are likely underestimates. [30] The fluke indicates that mollusks were eaten, not which mollusks. Wild black carp demonstrably eat native unionids; neither study measures population-level loss.

**Established versus hypothesized.**

| Status | Claim |
|---|---|
| Established | Black carp are established and reproducing in parts of the Mississippi basin. [7, 10] |
| Established | Wild black carp consume native unionids; mollusk consumption is common and probably under-recorded by stomach contents alone. [24, 30] |
| Established | Juvenile native mussels and unattached zebra mussels are vulnerable in controlled feeding trials; attachment and thick shells reduce vulnerability. [9, 23] |
| Established | Native unionid renewal is host-dependent and, for several species, slow to reach maturity; dreissenid renewal is host-free and fast. [20–22] |
| Hypothesized | Where black carp overlap native populations already suffering restricted recruitment, their predation compounds the existing replacement deficit. |
| Hypothesized | Dreissenid recruitment currently outpaces black carp predation in specific overlapping reaches. |
| Hypothesized | Abundant dreissenids sustain or attract black carp in ways that raise predation on co-occurring natives (apparent competition). [25] |

The sources reviewed here do not document host obstruction, dreissenid fouling, and black carp predation acting on the same native population at the same time. The combined scenario is a predicted interaction, not a documented event.

**Mechanism (interpretation, not measurement).**

1. *Unequal consequence does not require equal predation.* Prey use by black carp is opportunistic and habitat-dependent, so natives and dreissenids are unlikely to be taken at equal rates. The argument does not need them to be. Demographic consequence depends on added loss **relative to realized recruitment**. A modest loss rate can exceed the replacement capacity of a host-limited, slow-maturing population while a larger loss rate is absorbed by a host-free, fast-maturing one.
2. *Two pathways to the same exit.* Predation on native recruits deepens the reproductive debt described in §2 while the adult stock continues to conceal it. Predation on native adults consumes the standing stock that does the concealing. In HKL terms, the first lowers future renewal and the second spends the remaining buffer; both shorten the time to first exit from a declared viable region.
3. *Apparent competition.* Classic theory shows that a predator sustained or attracted by an abundant, fast-renewing prey can depress a co-occurring slow-renewing prey even when the two prey never compete directly. [25] Abundant dreissenids could, in principle, play that role for black carp. Whether this occurs in the Mississippi basin is **untested**.
4. *Selectivity among invaders.* Attachment protects zebra mussels in part, and suppression of a fast-renewing prey may be transient while losses of slow-renewing natives accumulate. The thick-shelled invasive *Corbicula*, whose abundance has been negatively related to native juvenile growth in the wild, is the prey juvenile black carp handled worst. [9, 26] A shared predator need not fall evenly even among the invaders.

**Limits.** No field estimate of black-carp-attributable native mortality or recruitment loss exists here, and no evidence yet shows that dreissenid recruitment exceeds black carp predation in a particular overlapping population. Spatial overlap among black carp, dense dreissenid populations, and vulnerable native beds must be established before a basin-scale interaction is inferred. Density-dependent fertilization is **not** invoked to strengthen the argument; experimental evidence on reproductive Allee effects in unionids is mixed. [27]

## 4. Managed juvenile rearing as evidence of corrective dependency

The original unionid reproductive cycle requires no hatchery operator to capture a fish, arrange a controlled larval encounter, or keep juveniles in a managed culture system. Where native populations or reproductive pathways have been impaired, conservation practitioners may do exactly those things. This is the most direct practical connection between **lost naturally available response pathways** and **deliberate corrective work**. [3, 5, 6]

**Actual propagation process.** Virginia Tech's Freshwater Mollusk Conservation Center describes collecting suitable host fish, nonlethally collecting glochidia from gravid female mussels, exposing host fish in aerated containers, holding infected fish while larvae transform, and culturing newly detached juveniles until they are suitable for stocking. The facility describes typical post-transformation rearing periods of roughly one to two years, varying by species and program. This process *uses living hosts*; it does not imply that hatcheries manufacture an equivalent fish-free reproductive mechanism. [6]

**Doing some of the "skipjack's work."** Hart and colleagues (2018) confirmed skipjack herring as a host for ebonyshell and elephant-ear mussels and developed methods for difficult-to-hold host fish, including experiments using excised infected gills after an initial period on live fish. This is an especially concrete case of scientific work to recover, reproduce, or study a biological dependency whose natural operation has been obstructed in some locations. It is **not evidence that all hatcheries routinely raise ebonyshell using skipjack**. [5]

**Protected cages and silos.** The National Mississippi River Museum & Aquarium documents floating culture cages containing inoculated host fish; these help concentrate metamorphosed juveniles and exclude predators. An experimental study in Kentucky placed juveniles in silos and sediment cages to examine growth and survival under differing conditions. These devices represent controlled exposures or protective culture, not proof of universal survival or repopulation. [14, 16]

**Important limitation:** An ordinary cage is **not** automatically zebra-mussel-proof. Researchers explicitly measured dreissenid biofouling on native unionids suspended in cages in Great Lakes nearshore habitats. Protection from large fish and protection from microscopic zebra-mussel settlement are different engineering requirements. Likewise, absent an identified project, do not state that a specific enclosure was built to exclude black carp. [8, 14, 16]

This distinction matters to the thesis: an introduced stressor can complicate the *corrective architecture itself*. A protected juvenile program may require site selection, host handling, aquatic husbandry, monitoring, and mitigation of fouling or predation, all of which are observable resource commitments. Whether the effort achieves sustainable native reproduction following release remains a separate empirical question. [5, 6, 8, 14]

## 5. Capacity lost beyond the harvested organisms

Living mussel beds and their shells provide habitat structure, filter suspended organic matter, redistribute nutrients by biodeposition and excretion, and can influence benthic algae and food webs. Nutrient recycling is **not** synonymous with permanent nitrogen or phosphorus removal; some nutrients are retained, some relocated, some excreted, and some transformed by microbial processes. [11, 13]

The Kiamichi River study followed native mussel populations through two decades and used species-specific physiological rates and biomass estimates to model declining biofiltration, nutrient recycling, and nutrient storage as populations declined. Its **method and mechanism** are useful here; its calculated magnitudes should not be transplanted into the Ohio–Mississippi basin. Experiments also demonstrate functional guild differences with temperature, so loss of species can remove environmental response options beyond the loss of aggregate biomass. [11, 12]

For this worked example, the warranted conclusion is that **native filtration, nutrient cycling, habitat-forming capacity, and reproductive options were reduced or altered in documented impacted settings**. It is not warranted to infer that every invaded reach became muddier, that all filtration disappeared, or that every downstream fish or benthic decline was caused by mussel depletion. [11–13]

## 6. Translation into HKL's existing terms

**OIE — process, not scalar.** Commercial extraction, river engineering, human-mediated zebra-mussel distribution, and black-carp introduction are candidate agentic processes capable of generating or transferring destabilizing burden. They should be causally attributed by action and receiving dependency; subsequent ecological consequences need not be relabeled as independent human decisions. [15] The processes differ in how their burden is delivered over time: harvest acts while the activity continues; a dam injects burden continuously, over a wide reach, for as long as the structure stands; an escaped, reproducing introduction keeps generating burden after the originating act has ended. The burden-generation *rates* of each are spatially distributed; the OIE itself remains the attributed process (see §8, item 2). [7, 15]

**Temporal decoupling.** Commercial utilization can realize immediate returns while replenishment occurs much more slowly. In the Keokuk mechanism, adult survival can postpone the visible effects of recruitment failure. A dated contrast between industrial and ecological timelines: an Iowa DNR historical timeline records the 1913 Keokuk dam as preventing skipjack herring from migrating upstream and thereby eliminating ebonyshell, the button industry's preferred mussel, from the Upper Mississippi, and records peak button production in 1916. [28] The two timelines did not move together. The dates do **not** show that the shells supporting 1916 output came from the populations whose renewal had been obstructed upstream; that linkage would require species- and reach-specific harvest records. The secondary summaries cited here (NPS, Iowa DNR) draw on the same historical literature rather than confirming one another independently; the contemporaneous U.S. Bureau of Fisheries studies of the Keokuk dam are the primary source to consult. [33] The National Park Service notes that conditions allowing a significant skipjack migration past the dam have occurred only once (1986) since its completion, so upper-river renewal is effectively episodic rather than strictly zero. [4] This is analogous in structure—not identical in material cause—to the Central Valley protocol's distinction between sustained delivered service and depletion of the stock supporting it. [4, 17]

**Renewal-rate asymmetry.** §3.3 is the same structure expressed through mortality rather than extraction: one burden source acting on two stocks with very different realized renewal rates accumulates burden only where added loss outruns renewal. Dams impair native renewal (persistent OIE); black carp predation then adds loss to recruits, which deepens reproductive debt, and to adults, which spends the standing buffer (self-propagating OIE). [3–5, 9, 20–23, 29, 30]

**Established concepts.** The distinction this report relies on—a stock that persists while the processes renewing it have failed—is not original to HKL. Ecology describes it as *extinction debt* (species or populations committed to eventual loss by past habitat destruction but not yet gone) and *functional extinction* (a population that can no longer reproduce, as estimated for the Chinese paddlefish by 1993). [35, 39] Fisheries work on Yangtze dams describes dam-induced breaks in life-cycle integrity as producing “invalid stocks.” [40] HKL's proposed contribution is narrower: to place that distinction inside a burden-and-correction accounting (sink impairment, temporal classes of OIE, corrective dependency; §8) that can be applied across domains. Whether that accounting predicts or explains outcomes better than these existing frameworks is the open test, not something this case settles.

**Regulatory variety.** The loss of species with distinguishable thermal or nutritional roles and the obstruction of host-dependent recruitment narrow some *available native response pathways*. Species count alone is not a universal measure of Ashby-style requisite variety. Describe which response functions were lost before claiming anything about their magnitude or the system-wide capacity to maintain viability. [12, 13, 18]

**PAE — intended corrective effort versus achieved correction.** Captive host infection, juvenile culture, protection, relocation, fish passage, and other restoration activities constitute examples of attempted elective correction when directed toward a declared ecological burden. They do not become measured "landed correction" merely because money, labor, or energy was expended. Passive recovery and continuing invasive pressure must be assessed separately. [6, 14, 15]

**Sahel comparison.** The Sahel example asks whether restoration aligns local knowledge, ecological conditions, control, and implementation. In the mussel case, matching the actual host fish, site, season, predator exposure, water quality, and habitat conditions similarly matters to whether corrective work lands. This is a structural comparison, not a claim that either case verifies the other. [18]

**Hydrological connections.** Dams, changing flows, sedimentation, and nutrient transport belong in the mussel case because they directly alter mussel viability and ecosystem services. They also open a cross-case comparison with groundwater extraction and connected waters. **Beaver-mediated hydrology is excluded** from this report and reserved for a separate case study. [4, 11, 17]

## 7. Boundaries, contrary findings, and publication rules

The case is a **source-qualified illustration**, not a pre-registered causal experiment. The following qualifications preserve its value:

- Human harvest did not explain all extirpations; dam impacts, pollution, drought, pathogens, and introductions differ by location. The causes of many enigmatic mussel declines remain unresolved. [1, 2, 19]
- Protected culture is not guaranteed recovery. Reintroduced juveniles may fail if natural habitat or host-fish access remains unsuitable. [6, 14, 16]
- Zebra mussels can continue filtration, and fish predators may sometimes suppress zebra mussels. Functional *replacement* is not equivalent to reinstating the previous assemblage. [8, 13]
- Black carp have demonstrated predation capability and established reproduction in parts of the basin, but a directly quantified regional contribution to native mussel losses is not established here. [7, 9, 10]
- The renewal-asymmetry argument in §3.3 is structural. Field predation rates, spatial overlap among predator and prey populations, and any apparent-competition effect remain to be measured. [23–25]
- No universal percentages for lost filtration, nutrient abatement, or requisite variety are inferred. No HKL burden score, ACR, or proof of universal OIE–PAE inequality is claimed. [15]

## 8. Definition questions this case raises for the registry

This worked example surfaces situations that Registry v0.3.1 cannot yet describe consistently. They are listed as **proposals for a future definition decision**, not adopted definitions. [15, 17]

1. **Sink impairment.** In the AEF burden balance, natural recruitment is passive removal ($\dot B_{pass}$). Harvest adds burden; a dam instead removes a passive sink. AEF guards against crediting passive recovery to correction but has no rule for attributing destroyed passive capacity to OIE. Because ACR counts only correction of channels present in its OIE denominator, a hatchery program would otherwise correct an "unrelated" burden. A declared rule is needed: either record $\dot B_{pass}$ at its unimpaired counterfactual and attribute the shortfall to $\dot B_{OIE}$, or the reverse—never both.
2. **Temporal classes of OIE.** *Pulse* OIE ends when the activity stops (harvest). *Persistent* OIE continues as long as an artifact stands and ends when it is removed (a dam). *Self-propagating* OIE persists and may grow after the originating act has ended (an escaped, reproducing introduction). Each realizes as spatially distributed burden rates; the class determines which corrections are even available—cessation, removal, or only containment—and how long attribution runs. The registry needs a rule for when a self-propagating population's ongoing burden stops being attributed to the originating act, because that choice changes any ACR denominator by orders of magnitude.
3. **Severed renewal.** $\rho_{TM}=\tau_s/\tau_d$ is defined only for positive, finite timescales. Upper-river ebonyshell renewal after 1913 is effectively zero apart from rare high-water passage, so the most extreme Temporal Mining instance in the corpus cannot be expressed as a finite ratio. Expressing Temporal Mining in rates (renewal rate may be zero or episodic) or admitting $\tau_s=\infty$ explicitly would resolve this. [4]
4. **Corrective dependency.** The title concept is not registered. A candidate definition using existing objects: compare first-exit buffer under a scenario that continues corrective expenditure ($\pi_{cont}$) with one that stops it ($\pi_{stop}$). A system is correctively dependent over a horizon when $\tau^{\pi_{stop}}_{buf}$ is finite and materially shorter than $\tau^{\pi_{cont}}_{buf}$, and the gap arises from impaired passive removal. This separates *pathway-restoring* correction (fish passage, host access), which should lengthen $\tau^{\pi_{stop}}_{buf}$ after a program ends, from *substituting* correction (rearing and stocking), which should not. Comparing sites of each kind after programs end is a test that could show the distinction adds nothing. The existence of a hatchery program shows that correction is being attempted; it does not by itself show that recovery would fail without it. The Keokuk case itself could become a prospective test: as of 2023 the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers was preparing a fish passage at the dam, described as the first of its kind on the Mississippi River. [38] If built, monitoring skipjack passage and subsequent ebonyshell recruitment upstream would directly test whether restoring the host pathway restores autonomous renewal. (Current construction status was not verified for this revision.)

   *Cross-basin analog (outside this report's boundary).* The Yangtze already supplies operational versions of this test. For Chinese sturgeon, whose migration the Gezhouba Dam blocked, a 2026 population model found that roughly 40 years of artificial propagation and release delayed the disappearance of the spawning-migration population by about 31 years but delayed the failure of natural spawning by only about one year: the stock was maintained while its renewal was not. If releases stop, the model projects natural spawning failing again about 33 years later and the spawning-migration population being lost after about 41 years—an explicit $\pi_{stop}$ scenario. In a second scenario, completing water-temperature regulation and spawning-ground restoration, combined with continued juvenile releases managed for adaptation to post-dam conditions, could allow a small sustainable population whose size is capped by spawning-ground capacity. [34] In that model, substitution alone maintains the stock; only substitution combined with restoration of the impaired pathway offers a route back to autonomous renewal. The Chinese paddlefish, for which no captive population was ever established, lost reproduction first and was later declared extinct, the endpoint when a severed pathway has no substitute. [35] In April 2025, twenty hatchery-bred Yangtze sturgeon released into an engineered spawning ground on the Chishui River, an upper-Yangtze tributary without mainstem dams, spawned and produced hatched larvae—the species' first recorded natural reproduction since 2000—whereas earlier spawning attempts in the dammed mainstem produced eggs that failed to hatch. [36, 37] That contrast separates restored spawning *conditions* from a self-sustaining population, which would require wild-born fish to mature and reproduce. One component does **not** transfer: black carp are native to the Yangtze, so the invasive-predator pressure in §3.3 has no direct counterpart there.

5. **Coupled renewal.** The freshwater protocol treats recharge as exogenous to the stock. Native recruitment depends on the adult stock *and* on a second stock (host fish) whose access can be severed independently. The protocol template does not transfer without a coupled-stock term.
6. **Channel choice.** A burden channel defined as filtration or water-clarity service would register dreissenid filtration as partial recovery while the native assemblage declines. Native composition and recruitment should be declared hard constraints, consistent with HKL's rule that aggregate improvement cannot offset a violated hard constraint. [8, 13]

## 9. Conclusion

The historical record and modern experimental evidence together support a modest but meaningful conclusion: **several human activities depleted native mussel stocks, interrupted or complicated native reproductive pathways, and altered functions previously supplied by diverse, self-maintaining assemblages**. An added introduced molluscivore, black carp, demonstrably consumes native unionids in the wild, increasing concern in affected portions of the Mississippi basin. Because native realized recruitment is host-dependent and often slow while dreissenid recruitment is host-free and fast, a shared predator is expected to impose the more persistent consequence on the stock least able to replace its losses, whether it takes recruits or adults. That expectation is structural and remains to be measured in the field. [1–13, 20–25, 29, 30]

The central distinction this case supports is that **a system may retain visible biological stock and some ecological function while losing the causal pathways necessary for autonomous renewal**. The Keokuk–skipjack case shows that especially clearly.

At the same time, conservation programs now intentionally select host fish, manage glochidia, rear juveniles, protect them in culture systems, and intervene in altered river environments. Those actions make the cost of reconstructing a disrupted dependency visible. The empirical facts do **not** prove that all such programs are ineffective or permanent; they demonstrate the need for corrective effort and the constraints under which it must operate. [5, 6, 8, 14, 16]

Within HKL, this is an example of **buffer depletion, temporally delayed reproductive consequences, loss of some native functional response options, and subsequent requirements for targeted corrective effort**. The comparative work on Sahel regulation and Central Valley temporal mining supplies conceptual neighboring cases, not evidentiary substitutes. [15, 17, 18]
